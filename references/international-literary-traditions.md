# International Literary Traditions: Working Reference Map

**Research status:** Curated starting map, not a claim that every book below has been read in full. The confirmed claims in this file draw on the cited academic and institutional references. Specific interpretations must be checked against a text or scholarly study before being used as authoritative criticism.

**Scope:** Japanese, Russian, and French fiction and theory are emphasized here because they are particularly useful comparative cases. No national literature has a single essential style. The project also covers Chinese and other traditions.

## Method: Compare problems, not countries

When using a classic as a model, write down:
1. **The manuscript problem:** e.g., the reader needs to understand a betrayal without a direct confession.
2. **The reference:** which specific work and feature are relevant?
3. **The analytical operation:** which scene, voice, temporal choice, or relation could be inspected or tested?
4. **An alternative:** a different work or method that handles the problem differently.
5. **The trade-off:** what might be lost if that method were imported?
6. **Verification status:** directly examined primary text, supported by scholarship, or preliminary research lead.

A famous book is not automatically a source of general-purpose writing laws.

## A. Japanese literature

### A1. Murasaki Shikibu — *The Tale of Genji* / 源氏物語

**Question:** How can relationships remain psychologically legible across time, changing social positions, and incomplete information?

- **Method to test:** Track a relationship's history in layers: public role, private feeling, social obligation, reputation, and what other participants know.
- **Aesthetic context:** Motoori Norinaga's *mono no aware* reading emphasizes the emotional depth of transience and feeling, not the requirement that literature issue simple moral judgments.
- **Caution:** Norinaga's interpretation was developed in a particular historical intellectual context; it is not an exhaustive description of Japanese literature or the novel's reception.
- **Research anchor:** Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, [Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/) and [The Kokugaku School](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kokugaku-school/).

### A2. Natsume Sōseki — *Kokoro* / こころ

**Question:** What changes when the reader learns a character's motive only through a later confession?

- **Method to test:** Build two knowledge timelines: what the narrator believes during earlier scenes, and what a later account reveals. Track which early details become newly significant.
- **Potential application:** guilt, friendship, betrayal, generational tension, and unreliable self-interpretation.
- **Trade-off:** Late revelation can deepen re-reading, but may feel artificially withheld if the narrative viewpoint had no convincing reason to conceal it.
- **Research anchor:** [Natsume Sōseki Memorial Museum — Works](https://soseki-museum.jp/eng/soseki-natsume/work-introduction/), which documents the three-part structure and confession.

### A3. Akutagawa Ryūnosuke — *In a Grove* / 藪の中

**Question:** How can conflicting testimonies change the reader's relation to truth?

- **Method to test:** Give different narrators plausible stakes, memory limits, self-serving explanations, and contradictory accounts. Do not confuse "multiple points of view" with automatically equally valid or truthful testimony.
- **Trade-off:** Unresolved truth can be productive, but only where the uncertainty serves the book's purpose.
- **Verification:** Classic reading lead; inspect an authoritative edition before attributing precise formal devices or quoting.

### A4. Tanizaki Jun'ichirō — *The Makioka Sisters* / 細雪

**Question:** How can social change be made visible through family relationships, rituals, material objects, and everyday decisions?

- **Method to test:** Map how family rank, marriage negotiations, money, social expectations, and seemingly minor scenes produce cumulative consequences.
- **Trade-off:** Detailed social texture without selective emphasis can become inert exposition.
- **Verification:** Classic reading lead; confirm specific interpretations by reading relevant chapters.

### A5. Kawabata Yasunari — *Snow Country* / 雪国

**Question:** Can image, landscape, interpersonal distance, and gaps between meetings carry emotional narrative weight?

- **Method to test:** Compare an explicit emotional explanation with a version that conveys distance through sensory detail and situation.
- **Important caution:** A separate Stanford study documents Kawabata's experimental modernist and journalistic dimensions, challenging any reductive claim that he represents a timeless national style.
- **Research anchor:** Stanford Humanities Center, [Newspaper Journalism, Realism, and Modernism in Japan](https://shc.stanford.edu/arcade/publications/dibur/peripheral-modernisms/newspaper-journalism-realism-and-modernism-japan).

### A6. Popular period fiction — *jidai shōsetsu* / 時代小説

**Question:** How can historically situated fiction build forward momentum through serialized incidents and an expanding social world?

- **Method to test:** Analyze an episode's self-contained payoff, the unanswered problem that carries forward, and the social or ideological imagination of its historical setting.
- **Counterexample to stereotype:** Japanese fiction also includes popular, plot-driven historical adventure, not only understated psychological realism.
- **Research anchor:** James Reichert, [Literature for the Masses: Japanese Period Fiction, 1913–1941](https://humsci.stanford.edu/feature/literature-masses-james-reichert-east-asian-languages-and-cultures).

## B. Russian literature and literary theory

### B1. Leo Tolstoy — *Anna Karenina*; *War and Peace*

**Question:** How can private choices interact with a social and historical system without reducing characters to representatives of ideology?

- **Method to test:** Separate a major event into institutional conditions, other people's strategies, the protagonist's limited perception, and the resulting personal costs.
- **Trade-off:** Sweeping social explanation can diminish the immediacy of a character's subjective experience.
- **Verification:** Primary-text comparison required for specific scene-level conclusions.

### B2. Fyodor Dostoevsky — *Crime and Punishment*; *The Brothers Karamazov*

**Question:** How can distinct moral or intellectual positions coexist without one voice becoming a straw man?

- **Method to test:** Write an argument from each character's strongest available premises; give each speaker lived stakes and the ability to challenge the others.
- **Theoretical context:** Bakhtin's polyphony/dialogism identifies novelistic possibilities beyond a single authoritative voice.
- **Caution:** Even Bakhtin's application to Dostoevsky is debated; polyphony is not a requirement for every great novel.
- **Research anchors:** Caryl Emerson, [Oxford Academic chapter on Bakhtin's polyphonic novel](https://academic.oup.com/book/35229/chapter-abstract/299758641) and [Dickinson encyclopedia entry on Bakhtin](https://filosofia.dickinson.edu/encyclopedia/bakhtin-mikhail/).

### B3. Anton Chekhov — Selected short stories

**Question:** When can an apparently minor incident reveal a life without a dramatic resolution?

- **Method to test:** Identify the change in attention, relationship, self-knowledge, or possibility rather than imposing a twist.
- **Trade-off:** An open ending must still create an experience; vagueness alone is not profundity.
- **Verification:** Choose and read a specific story before making detailed claims.

### B4. Viktor Shklovsky — Defamiliarization / ostranenie

**Question:** How does a literary form make ordinary perception feel newly vivid?

- **Method to test:** Rewrite a familiar action from an unaccustomed but plausible viewpoint, through unusual temporal expansion or specific observation; compare clarity and effect.
- **Trade-off:** Constant perceptual estrangement can exhaust readers and flatten emphasis.
- **Research anchors:** [Poetry Foundation — Russian Formalism](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/education/glossary/formalism-russian); [Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism — Shklovsky](https://www.rem.routledge.com/articles/shklovsky-viktor-1893-1984).

### B5. Mikhail Bakhtin — Dialogism and polyphony

**Question:** Does each major character or social group possess a meaningful way of speaking, interpreting, and contesting the world?

- **Method to test:** Build a map of competing discourses—family, class, occupation, ideology, private speech—and examine whether the narration imposes a single final verdict.
- **Trade-off:** Differentiated voices do not require every opinion to be equally credible or morally endorsed.
- **Research anchor:** [The Living Handbook of Narratology — Dialogism](https://www-archiv.fdm.uni-hamburg.de/lhn/node/67/revisions/67/view.html).

### B6. Vladimir Propp — *Morphology of the Folktale*

**Question:** What patterned functions organize a selected body of folktales?

- **Method to test:** Compare functions in a tale or folktale-inspired work; do not mechanically impose the morphology on modern psychological or literary fiction.
- **Verification:** Read a suitable scholarly edition for precise functions and terminology.

## C. French literature and narratology

### C1. Gustave Flaubert — *Madame Bovary*

**Question:** How can narration expose the gap between a character's desires and social realities?

- **Method to test:** Examine free indirect discourse and the distance between what a character thinks a scene means and what surrounding details suggest.
- **Trade-off:** Irony can sharpen insight but may undermine empathy if made uniformly contemptuous.
- **Verification:** Close reading of an edition is required before quoting or attributing a sentence.

### C2. Honoré de Balzac — *La Comédie humaine*

**Question:** Can a fictional society gain depth through linked institutions, professions, money, property, and recurring figures?

- **Method to test:** Link a character's personal decisions to the prices, incentives, offices, status structures, and material conditions shaping choices.
- **Trade-off:** World information is not automatically story information; select details with consequences.

### C3. Marcel Proust — *In Search of Lost Time*

**Question:** How can recollection change what an earlier event means, instead of merely supplying backstory?

- **Method to test:** Write an event once in immediate perception, then revisit it through transformed knowledge and desire.
- **Trade-off:** Memory writing needs narrative pressure or insight; temporal complexity alone does not create depth.

### C4. Albert Camus — *The Stranger*; Marguerite Duras — *The Lover*

**Question:** How does sparse narration, displacement, or fragmented recollection change moral and emotional interpretation?

- **Method to test:** Compare a fully explained scene with selective omission; assess whether the reader gains productive interpretive freedom or merely loses necessary information.
- **Caution:** The two authors serve different projects; do not bundle their styles into a uniform "French minimalism."

### C5. Gérard Genette — *Narrative Discourse*

**Question:** How does presentation transform the reader's experience of events?

- **Operational categories:** Order (analepsis/prolepsis), duration (scene/summary/pause/ellipsis), frequency, mood/focalization, and voice.
- **Method to test:** Diagram chronological events and compare them with their narrated order; mark repetitions, gaps, and viewpoint shifts.
- **Trade-off:** The categories describe narrative organization; they do not dictate how a good novel must be structured.
- **Research anchors:** Harvard literary scholar Amanda Claybaugh, [How to Read a Novel](https://claybaugh.scholars.harvard.edu/pages/how-read-novel-0); [Narrative, Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/literature-and-arts/language-linguistics-and-literary-terms/language-and-linguistics/narrative).

### C6. Roland Barthes; Tzvetan Todorov

**Question:** What textual codes, expectations, and narrative grammar help readers make sense of stories?

- **Method to test:** Analyze an actual text's recurring signs and knowledge-release mechanisms before turning the findings into a writing hypothesis.
- **Caution:** Structural description can illuminate, but not replace, historical or psychological interpretation; Barthes and Todorov should not be treated as presenting a single identical theory.
- **Verification:** Further primary and scholarly reading required.

## D. Keep the canon open

This is **not** a claim that Japanese, Russian, and French works are superior or that these traditions explain global literary practice. Relevant comparative choices may also include:

- **Chinese:** *Dream of the Red Chamber*, *The Scholars*, Lu Xun, Shen Congwen, Zhang Ailing, Xiao Hong, Zhang Henshui.
- **Anglophone:** Austen, Eliot, Woolf, Faulkner, Morrison, Le Guin, genre and serial storytelling traditions.
- **Other traditions:** Latin American fiction, Arabic narrative and frame tales, African literatures, South Asian fiction, Indigenous and oral storytelling traditions, and world epics.

Expansion should be driven by specific questions and access to credible primary texts and scholarship, not arbitrary national quotas.

## E. Research integrity and next-stage work

Current page-level source checks support selected descriptions and theoretical distinctions above; these **do not constitute complete readings** of the novels or theories. Before claiming a detailed stylistic observation, read the relevant text. Before importing a psychological or cultural theory, check its limits and counterexamples.

### Working research questions

1. When does multi-voiced narration create genuine independence instead of a staged debate?
2. When does delayed confession deepen the story, and when does it merely manufacture a twist?
3. Can historical social detail carry plot causality without turning into exposition?
4. How do serialization, translation, and readership change standards of pacing and style?
5. When do silence and open endings create meaning rather than incompleteness?
6. How can the Skill handle a language's literary register without treating a translation as the original?
